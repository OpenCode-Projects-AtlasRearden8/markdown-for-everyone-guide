Lesson 8 — Quotes, Code, Horizontal Rules, and Escaping

A grab-bag of finishing touches. Small tools, but they show up constantly in notes, READMEs, and documentation.

Blockquotes: for borrowed words and warnings

Start lines with > (greater-than, space):

> Not everything that counts can be counted.
> — often credited to Albert Einstein

Not everything that counts can be counted. — often credited to Albert Einstein

Great for: quotes, pull-out warnings in guides, answers in study notes. Consecutive > lines stay one quote; a blank line ends it.

Code: inline and fenced

Inline code — single backticks for a word or two:

Press `Ctrl+S` to save, or run `npm install`.

Renders: Press Ctrl+S to save, or run npm install.

Fenced code — triple backticks around a block:

```text
Project tree:

my-project/
├── README.md
├── docs/
└── images/
```

Fenced blocks keep every space and line break exactly as typed — that's why we've used them all through this guide to show you Markdown without rendering it.

Formatting a block of existing text: in VS Code, Obsidian, or GitHub's editor, select the lines and press backtick three times or use the language button — faster than typing fences manually.

Horizontal rules: for "chapter break" moments

Three hyphens on their own line (blank lines above and below):

---

New section below the line.

Use sparingly — a ## heading usually does the job better. Two or three rules per document is plenty.

Escaping: when Markdown eats your punctuation

Sometimes you want people to see a literal * or #. Put a backslash in front:

\* Not a bullet, just a star
\# Not a heading, just a hash
\_ Not italics

Renders as: * Not a bullet, just a star

The characters that need escaping most often: `, *, _, #, [, ].

Try it now

  1. Quote one sentence from a book you like.
  2. Put your keyboard's Save shortcut in inline code.
  3. Write a fenced block holding a tiny folder tree.
  4. Escape one * so it shows up as a star.
