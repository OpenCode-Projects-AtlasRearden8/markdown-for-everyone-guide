Lesson 4 — Lists: Bullets, Numbers, and Nested Steps

Lists are where Markdown starts paying rent. Grocery runs, care plans, packing bags, troubleshooting steps — anything with items belongs in a list.

Bulleted (unordered) lists

Start lines with - (hyphen, space). That's all:

- Milk
- Dog food
- Coffee filters
- Stamps
  • Milk
  • Dog food
  • Coffee filters
  • Stamps

* and + also work, but - is cleanest to read and most common.

Numbered (ordered) lists

Start lines with a number, a dot, and a space:

1. Preheat oven to 400°F.
2. Season the chicken.
3. Roast for 35 minutes.
  1. Preheat oven to 400°F.
  2. Season the chicken.
  3. Roast for 35 minutes.

Handy honesty: the numbers don't have to be correct. 3. 4. 5. renders as 1, 2, 3 — the order of the lines decides the numbers, so copy-pasted lists never come out wrong.

Nested lists (sub-items)

Indent two spaces (some apps want four; two spaces works on GitHub):

1. Get the car ready
   - Check tire pressure
   - Top off wiper fluid
2. Check in
   - License
   - Insurance card
   - Clinic card
  1. Get the car ready - Check tire pressure - Top off wiper fluid
  2. Check in - License - Insurance card - Clinic card

You can mix bullet types when nesting: numbers for main steps, hyphens for details. That mixing is a driver's-license-day classic.

Separating steps from stuff

This distinction is the whole game:

  • Numbered lists mean order matters — do step 1 before step 2.
  • Bulleted lists mean order doesn't matter — it's a pile of stuff.

If your "steps" are bullets, a reader may do them in the wrong order. If your "stuff" is numbered, a reader may think there's a required sequence. Choose on purpose.

Try it now

  1. Make a 4-item grocery list with -.
  2. Make a 3-step bedtime routine with 1. 2. 3..
  3. Nest two sub-items under step 2, indented two spaces.
